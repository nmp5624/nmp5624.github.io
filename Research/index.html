Labor Economics

  1. Corporal Punishment, Child Development and Household Labor Supply (2025)
    Nikita Pavlov
    Prospective Job Market Paper. First draft coming soon!

    I develop and estimate a dynamic model of child human capital formation that embeds parents’ option to implement a corporal-punishment (CP) scheme within the household’s intertemporal time budget and laborsupply problem. Parents allocate time budget across productive time with the child, leisure, and market work; the child acts as a Stackelberg follower, allocating time to self-investment, regular leisure, and non-compliant leisure (misbehavior). If adopted, the CP scheme governs realized punishment via a Poisson process whose intensity is proportional to non-compliant leisure. The scheme deters misbehavior but can harm the child’s cognitive capital and mental stability capital — two age-dependent, interrelated human-capital dimensions that enter both the parents’ and the child’s utilities. As an alternative to CP, parents can discourage misbehavior by spending more time with the child, thereby tightening the child’s time budget; this comes at the cost of foregone leisure and work hours, creating a non-trivial parenting-style trade-off. The model allows for rich heterogeneity in parental and child preferences and in labor-market opportunities across households, addressing selection on both sides. I estimate the model by the Method of Simulated Moments using the PSID (1986–2007) dataset. Counterfactual bans of CP indicate average gains of 0.12σ in final mental-stability capital among previously exposed children, rising to 0.20σ for those who also received physical punishment. The ban reduces mothers’ labor-force participation by 4% and, among stayers, cuts median hours by 14%, lowering median household income by 3.8%; fathers’ participation changes are negligible. Effects on cognitive capital are heterogeneous and often ambiguous, reflecting offsets between the ban’s direct benefits and indirect losses from reduced child self-investment and household income. Policy implications favor pairing CP restrictions with supports that preserve parental time and earnings.

  2. Household Transfers and Child Development: Effects on Cognitive and Non-Cognitive Skill Formation (2025)
    Nikita Pavlov, and Ewout Verriest
    First draft coming soon!

    Using the DFVW (2025) dynamic model of child human-capital formation, we compare transfer designs and their effects on children’s cognitive and non-cognitive skills. We contrast restricted transfers that must be spent on child-specific consumption with unrestricted cash given to parents, under both conditional and unconditional regimes. Across both regimes, restricted transfers yield larger gains in cognitive skills than unrestricted cash. However, these gains can come at a cost to non-cognitive skills when policies introduce strong extrinsic incentives that, in the model, depress the child’s effective discount factor and crowd out intrinsic motivation. Counterfactual simulations delineate when restricted transfers and conditionality raise cognitive achievement without eroding non-cognitive development, and inform policy design that balances improvements in cognition with the preservation of socio-emotional skills.

  3. From Rules to Hugs: The Impact of Parental Strictness and Warmth on Juvenile’s Time Allocation (2023)
    Nikita Pavlov
    3rd Year Paper
    PDF

    In this paper, I study the effects of parental strictness and warmth on juveniles’ time allocation and human capital formation. I build a structural model where a juvenile solves a time allocation problem by choosing how much time to spend on studying, delinquent leisure, and regular leisure. Her returns from studying and delinquent leisure depend on corresponding dimensions of human capital, namely educational capital and delinquent capital. I assume that parental strictness and warmth are capital inputs and use instrumental variables (IV) to estimate their impact on the corresponding measures of human capital. My estimates suggest that warmth has a strong positive impact on educational capital, whereas strictness has a strong negative impact on delinquent capital. Simulations reveal that although warmth does not discourage delinquent leisure as much as strictness does, it greatly incentivizes studying both through a direct effect on educational capital and through emerging time substitution patterns. At the same time, the strong decrease in delinquent leisure induced by strictness is compensated mostly by an increase in regular leisure rather than studying. The implications of these results for human capital formation are discussed.

Theoretical Econometrics

  1. Minimax regret treatment rules with finite samples when a quantile is the object of interest (2024)
    Patrik Guggenberger, Nihal Mehta, and Nikita Pavlov
    At consideration in Econometric Theory
    PDF

    Consider the setup in which a policymaker is informed about the population by a finite sample and based on that sample has to decide whether or not to apply a certain treatment to the population. We work out finite sample minimax regret treatment rules under various sampling schemes when outcomes are restricted onto the unit interval. In contrast to Stoye (2009) where the focus is on maximization of expected utility the focus here is instead on a particular quantile of the outcome distribution. We find that in the case where the sample consists of a fixed number of untreated and a fixed number of treated units, any treatment rule is minimax regret optimal. The same is true in the case of random treatment assignment in the sample with any assignment probability and in the case of testing an innovation when the known quantile of the untreated population equals 1/2. However if the known quantile exceeds 1/2 then never treating is the unique optimal rule and if it is smaller than 1/2 always treating is optimal. We also consider the case where a covariate is included.

  2. Asymptotically optimal statistical treatment rules when a quantile is the object of interest (2024)
    Nikita Pavlov
    Draft coming soon! Preview of the results in parametric setting:
    PDF

    Researchers are often presented with sufficient information to construct a class of consistent treatment assignment rules, all of which drive the loss function to zero in the limit. The seminal paper by Hirano and Porter (2009) develops large sample results to compare rules within such a class and derives treatment rules that are asymptotically optimal in the sense of minimizing mean sample loss. In this paper, I follow Hirano and Porter’s limiting experiment framework, but instead of the mean sample loss, I focus on an arbitrary quantile of the sample loss distribution. I derive asymptotically optimal Bayes and minimax sample quantile rules for some common classes of loss functions, both in parametric and semiparametric settings.
